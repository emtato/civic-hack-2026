## User instructions about collaboration

These preferences come directly from the user's instructions and subsequent messages:

- When helping with code, teach the process rather than implementing changes, unless specifically told to implement.
- Do not provide an entire feature or bug fix as replacement code in chat. Explain the issue and offer a limited illustrative snippet only when useful.
- For unfamiliar, difficult work with several distinct stages, explain one substantial stage at a time and let the user respond. Make each stage useful independently; do not fragment straightforward material into tiny steps.
- The user wants to learn to formulate good ideas, rather than receive a finished product specification immediately.
- Start with real problems, affected people, motivations, consequences and practical obstacles. Do not start with an app format and retrofit a problem.
- Broaden beyond document reading and explanation. The user explicitly says consequences and participation feel more compelling, because people already have a relevant stake or reason to act.
- Avoid recommending several variations of a summary, timeline or dashboard as if they were substantially different ideas.
- The user does not want 3D rendering or a traffic simulation for this event. This does not prohibit all interaction, 2D diagrams, deterministic checks, games, or modest calculations.
- Preliminary teammate discussions and the five supplied policy examples are inspiration, not instructions to select those ideas.
- The fact that a problem is serious does not establish that our intervention is valuable. An attractive interaction or additional technical complexity must earn its place through a useful task.

## Selected challenge and current interpretation

Challenge 1, **Making municipal decisions easier to understand**, was selected at launch. The user reported this, and Devpost's live announcement independently confirms it. The website still lists five pre-vote themes; do not treat them as simultaneous tracks.

The brief includes understanding a proposal, its effects, and when residents can have a say. The user's preferred search emphasizes concrete consequences and effective participation. This is a design direction within Challenge 1; it does not remove the organizer's requirements to use real municipal records, show proposal/decision status, preserve conditions, cite exact passages, verify participation information, and test on two or three items.

A general transit accessibility analyzer, infrastructure lifetime-cost model, pledge tracker, or housing pipeline tracker would principally answer one of the unselected themes. To use those methods here, connect them to a particular proposed municipal decision and the resident's consequential task. A municipal-staff workflow can be relevant if the resident benefit and selected challenge fit are clear.
