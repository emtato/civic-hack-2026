Tracking city commitments
How  might  we  help  residents  see  what  progress  a  municipality  has  made  on  a  public 
commitment, and what's still unclear?
Build a working proof of concept that a municipality could realistically pilot. It can be a web app, data  
tool, map, dashboard or another format. A municipal leader should be able to use it without your team  
in the room.
The problem
Municipalities make many public pledges (strategic plans, housing pledges, budget promises) and set  
targets, but residents can struggle to find out what has happened since. Progress information may be  
scattered, outdated, or difficult to compare with the original promise. Reporting could appear in annual  
reports, committee updates, budget documents, and the occasional dashboard, each with its own format  
and timeline. Targets also change. Deadlines shift, measures are redefined and plans are replaced, which  
makes it hard to tell whether a commitment is on track, delayed or quietly dropped.  Residents rarely get a 
clear answer to a simple question: what did the city promise, and is it happening?
A commitment needs a clear target, deadline and measure before progress can be assessed. For example,  
Toronto’s goals include reducing emissions from City-owned buildings by 60% from 2008 levels by 2030.  
Its facilities plan also sets a 37 MW renewable-energy goal for 2030. [1, 2] The City publishes utility data  
and an inventory of renewable installations, but the datasets have different coverage and limitations. An  
installation is evidence of an action; it does not by itself establish energy generated, emissions reduced or  
achievement of a corporate target. [3, 4]
The  federal  Housing  Accelerator  Fund  is  a  second  example.  According  to  the  Parliamentary  Budget 
Officer, CMHC had committed $4.37 billion across 230 agreements, in which governments agreed to carry 
out 1,744 initiatives toward a target of 112,000 additional homes by 2028. Pledges like these are published 
in different places and reported on different schedules, so a resident has no single place to see whether a  
promise is being kept.
Residents, journalists and community groups who want to hold a city to account must piece together  
evidence  from  scattered  documents.  Even  staff  can  find  it  hard  to  give  a  clear  status  across  many 
commitments and departments. 
There is a need for tools that link a commitment to its measures, show progress over time and are honest  
about gaps: where data  is  missing,  the measure is vague or  responsibility sits  with another  level of 
government. 
How common and persistent it is 
 In  2019,  municipalities  across  Ontario  and  Canada  declared  climate  emergencies.  Several, 
including Toronto, Mississauga, Ottawa and Vancouver, have since acknowledged they are not on  
track to meet their 2030 emissions targets.

Questions to investigate
 What happens when commitments outlive councils? Plans can cover decades but councils change  
every four years. 
 How  will  you  handle  inconsistency  in  reporting  and  lack  of  consensus  over  terms  like  ‘in 
progress’?
 Who is responsible for delivering and reporting on a commitment when several departments or  
partners are involved?
 What evidence distinguishes actions taken from measurable progress toward a target?
 How  can  reporting  make  delays,  setbacks,  missing  evidence  and  uncertainty  clear  and 
understandable?
 What can existing municipal dashboards teach us about tracking commitments across multiple  
plans, and where do gaps remain? 
What it costs 
 When residents can't see follow-through, they assume nothing happened. 
 Without clear tracking, councils find out too late that commitments are slipping. 
 Staff spend time rebuilding progress reports for each request, committee or audit.
Who you are designing for
Primary users. Residents seeking a clear account of what a municipality promised and what evidence  
shows about progress. Community organizations advocating for specific goals. 
Municipal  users.  Staff  responsible  for  delivering  commitments,  reporting  results  and  answering 
questions from council.
Other stakeholders.  Councillors, community organizations, journalists and agencies contributing to  
delivery. Partners such as chambers of commerce, school boards and nonprofits who share responsibility  
for outcomes. Funders and senior governments that attach conditions to commitments. 
What success looks like
 Users  can  find  the  original  commitment,  responsible  organization,  target  and  deadline  where 
available.
 Progress claims link to dated evidence and use clear, consistent status definitions.
 The tool makes missing information and uncertainty visible without treating them as proof of failure.  
Flags what is unclear, such as missing data, vague wording or no recent update

Constraints
 Preserve  the  original  commitment  and  record  revisions.  Distinguish  activity,  spending,  and 
outputs from the intended outcome.
 Use comparable measures, time periods and organizational boundaries. Do not calculate target  
progress without a suitable baseline.
 Distinguish “no recent evidence” from “no progress”. Show the measurement date as well as the  
publication or retrieval date.
 Do not attribute every change to municipal action. Explain important data gaps and other factors  
that could affect the result.
Two public datasets to explore
You may use other public data that better supports your solution. Record your sources and dates, and  
make data limitations visible.
1. Annual Energy Consumption City Owned and Operated Buildings ( City of Toronto data  
and downloads)
Provides property and utility meter records for a defined group of facilities. Compare energy use across  
consistent buildings and periods. The file has no direct emissions totals or established comparable 2008  
baseline, so energy trends alone cannot measure progress against the emissions target.
2. Renewable Energy Installations (City of Toronto data and downloads)
Includes installation locations, system types, dates and sizes.  Check technologies and units before adding  
capacities; installed capacity is not annual generation.
Other potential data sources are municipal strategic plans, climate action plans and progress reports;  
council minutes and staff reports; budget documents; existing municipal dashboards; open data portals.
Explain your thinking
Show who the tool serves, why you designed it this way and how you checked its outputs. Include clear  
instructions, source links and any assumptions. Explain what a municipality would need to test or add  
before piloting it. If you use AI, make its role and limitations clear.
