Finding gaps in transit access
How might we use public data to spot where transit schedules make it hard to reach essential  
destinations, and figure out which improvements matter most and what the trade-offs are,  
including first-mile/last-mile gaps?
Build a working proof of concept that a municipality could realistically pilot. It can be a web app, data  
tool, map, dashboard or another format. A municipal leader should be able to use it without your team  
in the room.
The problem
Transit access varies greatly between the neighbourhoods of a city. Some have far more access issues, and  
it is hard to surface where the available systems are falling short. A nearby stop may still leave people  
facing long waits, difficult transfers or a long walk before they can reach an essential destination. A bus  
stop near your home does not guarantee useful access. What matters is whether the schedule gets you to  
work, school, a clinic, a grocery store or childcare at the times you need, in a reasonable travel time.  
Coverage maps show where routes run, not whether trips are practical at 6 a.m., on Sundays or for shift  
workers. 
The journey to and from the stop matters too. Missing sidewalks, unsafe crossings, long walks, winter  
conditions and a lack of bike parking or on-demand options can make a nearby stop unusable. These are  
first-mile and last-mile gaps.
The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) and the International Transport  
Forum  assess  accessibility  by  the  destinations  people  can  reach  within  a  given  time.  This  makes 
frequency, connections, and walking access important alongside the location of routes and stops. [1]  
Statistics Canada calculates convenient access to public transit as the share of people living within 500  
metres, measured along the street network, of a transit stop. In 2023, the national figure was 75.1% across  
1,111 municipalities with available data, with Toronto, Montréal and Vancouver above 82%, and smaller  
metropolitan  areas  generally  lower  (Statistics  Canada,  The  Daily,  2023).  In  2026  Statistics  Canada 
published a newer table that breaks access down by distance, transit carrying capacity, geography and  
demographic characteristics (The Daily, June 2026).
The  TTC  publishes  scheduled  routes,  stops  and  trip  times  for  buses,  streetcars  and  subways.  These 
records support comparisons across locations and times of day, but do not show actual delays, crowding  
or every barrier along a walking route. [2] Being near a stop does not mean a bus comes often. A tool that  
shows service frequency and who is affected would add something the headline statistics cannot.
How common and persistent it is 
 A national study found nearly one million low-income Canadians live in urban areas with low  
transit accessibility, putting them at risk of "transport poverty" (Allen and Farber, 2019). 
 In the Toronto area alone, the same research team estimated more than 600,000 people struggle  
with both low income and poor transit options. 
 A Toronto study found lower-income inner suburbs had transit scores less than a third of more  
affluent neighbourhoods.

Questions to investigate
 How do neighbourhood density, land use and street design affect access to frequent transit?
 How  do transit agencies  balance frequent service  on  busy  routes with coverage across  more 
neighbourhoods, and who benefits from each approach?
 How long does it take residents to reach essential destinations at different times of day, including  
walking, waiting and transfers?
 Where do first-mile and last-mile gaps make a journey difficult, even when transit service is  
available nearby?
 How do transit needs differ for shift workers, caregivers, seniors, newcomers and other residents,  
and whose experiences might public data miss?
 Which improvements could make the greatest difference, and what trade-offs would they involve  
in cost, service frequency, coverage and accessibility?
What it costs 
 Economic factors include limited access to jobs which narrows employment options and can force  
households into costly car ownership. 
 Health and wellbeing in terms of missed medical appointments, harder access to fresh food, and  
social isolation, especially for seniors and people with disabilities. 
 Equity as the burden falls most heavily on low-income households, women balancing work and  
childcare, and recent immigrants. 
Who you are designing for
Primary users:  Transit planners and transportation staff investigating gaps and comparing possible  
service improvements.
Secondary  users:  Transit-dependent  residents,  including  students,  shift  workers,  older  adults, 
caregivers,  and  people  with  mobility  limitations.  Residents  of  fast-growing  suburban  areas  and  new 
subdivisions. 
Municipal users.  Equity, planning and sustainability staff who work on access to jobs, services, and  
housing which are all linked to mobility. City and regional councillors who need an easy-to-read evidence  
base.
Other stakeholders.  Neighbourhood groups, accessibility advocates, destination operators and transit  
agencies. 
What success looks like
You might build an access map, an understandable service score or a tool that compares improvements.  
Include first-mile and last-mile connections (the journey to the first stop and from the final stop to the  
destination).
 Users can see where and when access is weaker and understand the criteria behind that finding.  
Surfacing of specific places and times where access breaks down, not just general averages. 
 The analysis considers service frequency, waiting, transfers, and access to essential destinations.

 Explains results clearly for a non-technical audience .
Constraints
 Define what “frequent”, “accessible” or “underserved” means in your analysis. Show the travel times,  
dates and assumptions used.
 Use dates covered by the transit feed and account for service calendars. Scheduled service is not the  
same as reliable service.
 Make walking estimates and accessibility gaps visible. A straight-line connection does not establish a  
safe or wheelchair-accessible route.
 Do not generalize from a few sample locations to all residents. Population or equity claims need  
supporting geographic and demographic evidence.
Public datasets to explore
You may use other public data that better supports your solution. Record your sources and dates, and  
make data limitations visible.
1. Merged GTFS TTC Routes and Schedules (City of Toronto data and downloads)
Includes routes, stops, trips, stop times and service calendars. Use it to measure departures,  
scheduled connections and travel times. Join tables using route, trip, stop and service identifiers,  
and check the feed’s service period.
2. Library Branch General Information (City of Toronto data and downloads)
Includes branch locations, coordinates, opening hours and service features. Libraries provide a  
useful example of essential community destinations. Select physical branches and check opening  
hours; this dataset does not cover all essential services.
3. Grand River Transit GTFS feed   (Region of Waterloo open data; GTFS schedule, with route and 
stop data). A local, well-maintained feed. The feed is also listed on the  Mobility Database, and 
teams can swap in any city's GTFS feed.
4. Statistics  Canada,  Table  23-10-0314-01:  Access  to  public  transport  by  distance  and 
carrying  capacity,  geography,  gender,  selected  demographic  and  socio-economic 
characteristics, and count of public transport stops  (see the June 2026 release). Useful as a 
benchmark and for demographic context.
