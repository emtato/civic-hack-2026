Making long term infrastructure costs visible
How might we help residents weigh an infrastructure project's upfront price against its future  
maintenance, operating and environmental costs?
Build a working proof of concept that a municipality could realistically pilot. It can be a web app, data  
tool, map, dashboard or another format. A municipal leader should be able to use it without your team  
in the room.
The problem
When a municipality approves a new road, recreation centre or pipe network, public debate usually  
centres on the upfront capital cost; the price to build a road, arena, pipe, library or new subdivision's  
servicing. An infrastructure project’s initial price is easier to see than its future operating, maintenance  
and environmental costs. Councils and residents need a clearer picture of the trade-offs over decades. An  
asset's  operating,  maintenance,  renewal  and  eventual  replacement  costs  can  far  exceed  the  price  of 
building it. Those future costs land on property taxes and utility rates for decades. Environmental costs,  
such as emissions, energy use and climate risk, are even less visible.
Ontario’s  municipal  asset  management  framework  considers  infrastructure  across  its  lifecycle: 
construction, operation, maintenance, renewal and decommissioning.  It connects costs with service levels 
and risks. A lower initial price can therefore lead to different long-term costs. [1, 2] Many municipalities  
face large backlogs of ageing infrastructure.
Toronto  publishes  a  ten-year  capital  plan  and  utility  records  for  defined  City-owned  and  operated 
facilities. These can inform an infrastructure comparison, but do not provide a complete lifetime estimate.  
Future costs and environmental effects require clearly stated evidence and assumptions. [3, 4]
The long-term costs of a new project are rarely presented to residents in a way they can compare or  
understand.  When only the upfront price is visible, decisions can favour options that look cheap now but  
are  expensive  over  time.  Low-density  growth,  for  example,  can  require  more  roads  and  pipes  per 
household to maintain. Senior government grants often cover construction but not upkeep, shifting long-
term costs onto local taxpayers. 
Tools that show lifecycle costs clearly and fairly could improve public debate and help councils explain  
why a more expensive option today may cost less overall. 
How common and persistent it is 
 Canada's core public infrastructure has a replacement value of $2.1 trillion, and municipalities  
own about 62% of it (Statistics Canada, 2020 data). 
 About $265 billion of that infrastructure is in poor or very poor condition, roughly $16,250 per  
private dwelling. Roads make up nearly half of it. A further $196 billion is in unknown condition. 
 Many smaller Ontario municipalities report reinvesting well below their target rate. For example,  
Markstay-Warren reports an actual reinvestment rate of 0.39% against a 2.77% target (Markstay-
Warren's Asset Management Plan, 2024).

Questions to investigate
 How  are  construction,  operating,  maintenance  and  replacement  costs  accounted  for  across 
municipal budgets, and where is the connection between them difficult to see?
 How do grants, debt and other funding sources affect what a project costs residents upfront and  
over time?
 What long-term infrastructure responsibilities come with new development, and how are those  
costs considered when growth is approved?
 How  can  residents  and  decision-makers  weigh  costs  and  benefits  that  extend  across  several 
council terms and generations?
 What can asset management plans reveal about future costs, and how could that information  
become easier for residents to understand?
 How  should  comparisons  account  for  environmental  impacts  and  uncertainty  about  asset 
lifespan, future prices and maintenance needs?
 Is the publicly available information currently easy to access and understand?
What it costs 
 Higher long-term spending as deferred maintenance turns into more expensive full replacements.  
Huntsville's asset plan estimates proactive lifecycle management saves 45% annually on roads  
(Town of Huntsville's 2024 Asset Management Plan). 
 Service risks such as watermain breaks, road closures and facility shutdowns when assets fail. 
 Intergenerational fairness when today's decisions shift costs to future taxpayers. 
 Trust can be eroded when residents feel blindsided by tax and rate increases they didn't see  
coming.
Who you are designing for
Primary users.  Residents and ratepayers trying to understand infrastructure spending and long-term  
value.
Municipal users. Councillors, finance staff, asset managers and operations teams comparing investment  
options and making budget and capital-plan decisions. 
Other  stakeholders.  Facility  and  service  users,  future  residents  and  communities  affected  by 
construction, emissions or service changes.
What success looks like
 Users can compare upfront spending with operating, maintenance, renewal and end-of-life costs over  
a stated period.
 The tool makes service and environmental trade-offs understandable alongside financial costs.
 Users can change important assumptions and see how the comparison changes.
 A municipal leader can use the working tool independently and understand which conclusions are  
supported and which need further evidence.
 Lifecycle cost modelling: estimating costs over 25 to 100 years requires assumptions about asset life,  
maintenance cycles, inflation and discount rates

 Discounting as the choice of discount rate strongly shapes results; tools should make it visible and  
adjustable 
 Showing costs over time, per household or per year so non-experts can compare options.
 Assumptions that are visible, explained and adjustable.
Constraints
 Compare options that provide a clearly stated service. Identify the asset, study period, price year and  
costs included.
 Keep capital, operating and renewal costs separate and avoid double counting. Explain inflation or  
discounting if used.
 Show sourced values, estimates and assumptions clearly. Test uncertainty in major inputs such as  
energy prices, maintenance and asset life.
 Keep environmental measures understandable and consistent. Operational emissions do not capture  
every construction impact; explain any monetary value assigned to them.
Public datasets to explore
You may use other public data that better supports your solution. Record your sources and dates, and  
make data limitations visible.
1. Capital Budget and Plan by Ward (City of Toronto data and downloads)
Includes project and subproject names, annual allocations and ten-year totals. Amounts are in thousands  
of dollars. Use relevant capital spending as a starting point; a ten-year allocation is not necessarily the full  
project cost or actual spending.
2. Annual Energy Consumption City Owned and Operated Buildings ( City of Toronto data  
and downloads)
This file has property and utility meter tables linked by Portfolio Manager ID, with consumption, units,  
dates and costs where populated. It can support a facility operating baseline. Missing costs are not zero,  
and this file has no direct greenhouse gas totals.
3. Statistics Canada, Canada's Core Public Infrastructure Survey tables. 
Including Table 34-10-0284-01 (estimated replacement value by physical condition rating) and Table 34-
10-0289-01 (inventory by year of construction). See the table list in The Daily, October 2024 . Useful for  
realistic replacement values, condition mix and asset age by type.
4. Ontario Financial Information Return (FIR) data   (Ontario Ministry of Municipal Affairs and 
Housing; XLSX/CSV, with newer years on the FIR open data site). 
Includes municipal revenues, capital and operating expenses, reserves and tangible capital assets for  
every Ontario municipality.
Other potential sources are municipal asset management plans (required in Ontario; most are public  
PDFs with  replacement  costs,  condition  and  lifecycle needs);  capital  and operating budgets;  Ontario 
Financial Information Returns (FIR); Statistics Canada's Core Public Infrastructure Survey; Canadian  
Infrastructure Report Card.
