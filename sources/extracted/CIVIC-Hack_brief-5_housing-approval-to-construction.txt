From housing approval to construction
How  might  we  help  residents  and  decision-makers  understand  what  happens  between  a  
housing approval and construction, and where progress gets hard to track?
Build a working proof of concept that a municipality could realistically pilot. It can be a web app, data  
tool, map, dashboard or another format. A municipal leader should be able to use it without your team  
in the room.
The problem
Housing  is  one  of  the  most  pressing  issues  facing  Canadian  cities.  Public  debate  often  focuses  on  
approvals: how quickly councils approve new homes and how many units they approve. But an approval is 
not a home. After approval, a project typically moves through further stages, such as site plan approval,  
conditions, financing, pre-sales, building permits, servicing and construction.   Housing approval does not  
always lead quickly to construction. Residents and decision-makers need to understand the steps between  
them, where projects sit, and where progress becomes difficult to trace. Many approved units take years to 
break ground, and some never do. The reasons vary: interest rates, construction costs, market demand,  
servicing capacity, labour shortages and development charges among them.
For  example,  Toronto’s  Development  Pipeline  separates  planning  approval,  permit  application  and  
permit-issued stages. A project counted as approved may still need other approvals. A building permit  
allows work to proceed, but issuance alone does not confirm that physical construction has started. [1]
Construction also depends on financing, labour, materials, servicing and market conditions. The Bank of  
Canada  describes  how  borrowing  costs  and  project  viability  can  affect  whether  housing  proceeds.  
Planning and permit records cannot reveal all of these conditions for an individual project. [2]
The pipeline between approval and construction is hard to see. Data sits in different systems: planning  
applications, building permits, servicing agreements and housing starts. These systems often use different  
identifiers, and some steps depend on private decisions that are not public at all.   Without a clear view,  
residents may blame the wrong actor, and decision-makers may pull the wrong levers. Better tools could  
show where projects stall and why, so that policy responses target the real bottlenecks.
How common and persistent it is 
 In the Greater Toronto and Hamilton Area, CivicAction counted 2,220 approved projects (about  
1.2 million units) on hold in mid-2025, more than four times the number of active projects. That  
is roughly 12 approved homes for every one under construction. 
 As  of  December  31,  2025,  Toronto’s  development  pipeline  included  approximately  297,000  
residential units at the planning-approved stage and a further 65,000 at the building-permit-
issued stage (City of Toronto’s Development Activity Bulletin, 2025)
 Ontario housing starts hit their lowest level since 2009 in early 2025 (Financial Accountability  
Office). The province forecasts about 71,800 starts in 2025, well below the roughly 150,000 a year  
needed for its 1.5 million homes goal. 
 35 of Ontario's 50 largest municipalities missed their 2024 housing targets (STOREYS, 2025).

Questions to investigate
 Which actors and decisions shape the transition from approval to construction, and which parts  
can a municipality influence?
 Where  does  visibility  break  between  planning  approval,  permit  application,  permit  issuance, 
construction start and completion?
 How should records be connected when projects have multiple phases, addresses, applications,  
permit revisions, and identifiers?
 What can public records reveal about timing and status, and what remains private or unobserved,  
such as financing, pre-sales, ownership or market demand?
 What other explanations could produce the same signal as a long interval, missing record or  
permit mismatch?
 How  does  uncertainty affect municipal planning, funding accountability,  service delivery  and  
public communication?
What it costs 
 Housing affordability suffers as every stalled project is supply that isn't reaching renters and  
buyers. 
 Municipal funding as cities can lose provincial funding. Ottawa missed out on its Building Faster  
Fund allocation after reaching 63% of its 2024 target. 
 Infrastructure costs when municipalities may build servicing for growth that doesn't arrive on  
schedule. 
 Trust  can  be  eroded  and  blame  can  be  incorrectly  assigned  when  without  shared  data,  
municipalities, developers and the province point at each other. 
Who you are designing for
Primary  users.  Residents  waiting  for  housing  and  neighbours  seeking  to  understand  the  status  of 
approved developments.
Municipal users. Housing, planning and economic development staff tracking delivery and identifying  
projects that need follow-up.
Other stakeholders.  Developers, non-profit housing providers, building officials, utilities, lenders and  
councillors. Provincial and federal governments funding and setting housing target. 
What success looks like
 Users can follow projects through clearly defined approval, permitting, and construction stages.
 The tool connects project and unit information without counting the same development more than  
once.
 It  highlights  long  intervals,  missing  milestones,  and  possible  bottlenecks  while  distinguishing  
evidence from inference.
 A municipal leader can use the working tool independently to understand the pipeline and identify  
useful follow-up questions.

Constraints
 Keep milestones distinct. Planning approval, permit application, permit issuance, construction start  
and completion or occupancy are different events.
 Define projects, phases, and units consistently. Multiple addresses, approvals, and permit revisions  
can refer to the same development or to different phases.
 Use documented dates for intervals. An application received date is not an approval date, and a  
missing match is not proof of a construction delay.
 Separate evidence from inference. A long interval, missing record or permit status may warrant a  
question; it does not prove that a project has stalled or explain why.
 Respect  what  public  data  does  not  show.  Financing,  pre-sales,  ownership  decisions,  labour  
availability, material costs and servicing capacity may be incomplete or private.
Public datasets to explore
You may use other public data that better supports your solution. Record your sources and dates, and  
make data limitations visible.
1. Development Pipeline (City of Toronto data and downloads)
Includes pipeline status, application number, date received, address, description, proposed residential  
units and links to planning records. It is a rolling snapshot rather than a complete milestone history;  
proposed units are not completed homes.
2. Building Permits Active Permits (City of Toronto data and downloads)
Includes permit and revision identifiers, work type, addresses, application and issue dates, status and  
dwelling-unit  fields.  It  excludes  cleared  records,  and  permit  issuance  alone  does  not  establish  
construction. Related permits can contain overlapping unit counts.
3. Application Information Centre (City of Toronto planning applications)
Provides current information on active Community Planning, Committee of Adjustment and Toronto  
Local Appeal Body applications. It refreshes daily, and closed applications may not appear. 
Explain your thinking
Show who the tool serves, why you designed it this way and how you checked its outputs. Include clear  
instructions, source links and any assumptions. Explain what a municipality would need to test or add  
before piloting it. If you use AI, make its role and limitations clear.
