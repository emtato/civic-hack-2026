**CIVIC Hack 2026: choosing an idea and finishing a credible prototype**

**Historical first-pass guide. Its recommended renter brief and document-comparison shortlist were subsequently shelved.** Start with the [current event handoff](civic-hack-main-prompt-instructions.md) and [discussion context](discussion-context.md). The latest user direction prioritizes real consequences and participation barriers; this guide is retained as history rather than current advice. Event facts now have dedicated references in [overview](overview.md), [judging/submission](judging-and-submission.md), and [Challenge 1](challenge-1.md).

My first choice to investigate is a **renter-focused decision brief that preserves conditions and explains what changed**. My second is a **short interactive walkthrough of a real street proposal**. Treat these as candidates to test in the first 90 minutes, rather than commitments.

The useful question is: **What can a resident understand or do after using this that they currently struggle to accomplish?** Keep the answer specific enough to demonstrate on a real council item.

This guide applies only to Challenge 1. Event pages, alternative-product descriptions and the linked municipal sources were checked on November 12, 2026, Eastern time. It draws on the existing [HTV synthesis](../hackathon-investigation/events/htv11/findings/htv11-initial-hypotheses.md), which integrates the 133-project research capture and thirteen category comparisons, including 37 School of Cities entries. I reread that synthesis and six relevant project reports for this guide. This is an application of the saved research, not a fresh review of all 133 projects or evidence of why particular judges voted as they did.

**1. Start with the actual task and scoring**

The attached [Challenge 1 brief](sources/official/CIVIC-Hack_brief-1_municipal-decisions.pdf) calls for an understandable explanation of a real municipal decision: what is proposed and why, who and where it affects, its current stage, and when and how input is possible. It asks for exact source passages, preserved conditions, neutral language, and testing on two or three real items. These are the competition's requirements, distinct from the suggestions in this guide.

The [official rubric](https://fciprojects.ca/hackathons/2026-civic-hack/) allocates:

| Area | Points | Components |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Project | 40 | Stakeholders 10; functionality 15; design and feasibility 10; practical value 5 |
| Software | 20 | Requirements and UML 5; architecture and scalability rationale 5; code understanding 5; code/library/algorithm references 5 |
| Presentation | 40 | Municipal Readiness Report 20; demo video 20 |

The discussion's “60% isn't related to the idea” interpretation would lead you astray. The report needs a plausible municipal use, the video needs a useful working task, and the software explanation needs choices you understand. A strong problem and well-chosen scope supply the material for all three areas.

The published participant count does not tell us the number or quality of submitted teams. It is not a useful reason to lower the standard of the core workflow.

Three practical lessons follow. Familiar full-stack technology can contain substantial domain work. An existing product should trigger a same-task comparison before rejection. A game can fit, provided its experience helps explain a real decision and you can check what the player understood.

For this brief, useful technical depth could be correctly connecting an original recommendation, a committee amendment and a final decision, then tracing each explanation to its source. That difficulty arises directly from the resident's task.

**3. Run a 90-minute idea session before committing**

Use the following five steps. Give each step an output so the discussion moves forward.

**Minutes 0–15: identify moments of confusion.**

Each person privately writes three situations before the team debates solutions. Use:

> When [specific person] encounters [specific municipal proposal], they cannot answer [important question], because [particular barrier]. Today they use [workaround]. We could help them [observable task].

Example: a tenant reads that a redevelopment was approved but cannot tell which protections were approved, which depend on later steps, or where the document answers their concern. The task could be finding a source-supported explanation and a question to clarify with staff.

Generate across different failures, even within one policy area:

| Person | Relevance problem | Meaning problem | Process problem |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Renter | Does this concern my building? | Which conditions concern existing tenants? | Is this a recommendation or a final decision? |
| Bus passenger | Does this cover my part of the route? | What changes about the journey? | Which part of the design remains open? |
| Local business operator | Is my loading area affected? | What access arrangements are proposed? | Where is the verified route for comments? |

Output: six to nine problem statements, then three to investigate. Do not select solely by how dramatic the underlying policy dispute sounds.

**Minutes 15–40: inspect the real input.**

Open an official item and its linked report for each candidate. Locate its decision-making body, dated recommendation, current decision, affected place, important condition, and participation information. Try the difficult connection immediately: can you tell which version governs the explanation?

Two real items should be available, with a third for later verification. Curated inputs are a reasonable prototype boundary if disclosed; the distinctive processing and interaction must still work. If the value depends on processing unseen documents, reserve an unseen item for the final check.

Output: source links and a short manually checked account. If a necessary fact is missing, label it missing. Do not invent a deadline or infer one from a meeting date.

**Minutes 40–55: compare the closest existing way of doing the task.**

The official council pages already contain item histories and decisions. [Civic Dashboard](https://civicdashboard.ca/) offers council activity, participation guidance and voting history. [PoliAlerts](https://polialerts.com/coverage/toronto) advertises agenda and keyword alerts, summaries and consultation deadlines. Those product descriptions establish overlap; I have not tested their logged-in workflows or established what they lack.

Therefore, “a feed plus AI summaries plus notifications” is an insufficient differentiation claim by itself. Compare the same real task using the closest tool you can access. Write:

> Our candidate makes [particular task] easier or more reliable through [specific mechanism]. We will check that by [small comparison].

For example: preserve a conditional clause while explaining its consequence, or show precisely which substantive part changed between stages. These remain proposed advantages until tested.

**Minutes 55–75: sketch two different experiences for the strongest problem.**

One can be a practical brief; the other can be interactive. Describe the resident's input, what the tool transforms, what appears, and the useful endpoint. Show the source at the moment it supports a claim.

For the interactive version, identify its purpose: practice interpreting a decision, reveal a trade-off, or compare affected perspectives. Avoid an invented “best policy” score. A resident's informed question is a legitimate endpoint; the project need not resolve the policy dispute.

Output: two paper sketches or short walkthroughs. Choose one defining interaction. Extra features can wait.

**Minutes 75–90: make a provisional choice using evidence.**

| Check | Evidence to require |
| --- | --- |
| Someone has a reason to use it | A concrete event, concern or decision affecting that person |
| It fits Challenge 1 | Explains a real decision and its relevance, status and participation situation |
| The useful difference is visible | One same-task comparison you can demonstrate |
| The necessary input is available | Official documents for two or three cases |
| The core claim can be checked | A manual reference and a revealing difficult case |
| It fits the remaining time | A useful end-to-end task achievable before adding extras |

Choose the candidate with the strongest evidence and team interest. If a dependency fails, first try a different mechanism or smaller claim. Shelve it when the valuable part still depends on unavailable data or an unsupported prediction.

**4. Investigate these three candidates first**

| Candidate | Resident's task | Defining interaction | Main uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Renter-focused decision brief** | Understand a redevelopment's relevant conditions and what remains unresolved. | Select a real building/item, open a renter lens, inspect source-linked conditions, then see a verified participation route or a clear closed/unknown state. | Whether the source material is sufficiently complete and the explanation improves on existing tools without implying personal eligibility. |
| **B. What changed in this decision?** | Update their understanding after an amendment or later meeting. | Compare recommendation and decision, highlight a substantive change, and open both supporting passages. | Whether you can reliably match equivalent clauses and distinguish substantive changes from formatting. |
| **C. One street, different needs** | Understand how a real transit proposal creates different benefits and access constraints. | Walk through sourced changes from passenger, delivery and nearby-resident perspectives; finish with a question about the proposal. | Whether users learn something transferable and whether any modeled effects have a defensible basis. |

**A is my first investigation choice.** It has a clear reason to care and a bounded task. Its useful output can be “this condition is in the adopted decision; this detail is not established by the material we reviewed.” It should not declare a particular tenant eligible for assistance from a few profile answers.

**B is the strongest alternative if document versions are accessible.** Begin with one decision type and two stages. Explain the change in plain language. A generic text diff is only the starting machinery; the resident needs its procedural and practical meaning. A source-linked change card could eventually become A's defining interaction, but earn that expansion after the first workflow works.

**C preserves the good part of the game discussion.** Use a real proposal with a small set of sourced choices or design features. A short interaction placed alongside a municipal notice or used in a consultation workshop has a plausible distribution route. It need not persuade everyone to install a game. Test whether the participant can explain one genuine trade-off afterward; completing a level is insufficient evidence.

For the five policy topics you supplied, rental redevelopment and bus-lane decisions are the strongest starting investigations. Development-charge or stormwater proposals can fit if centered on an actual proposed change; explaining a fee does not require forecasting housing prices or flood damages. Construction coordination needs a specific proposed municipal decision to explain, otherwise it drifts toward project tracking.

**6. Build toward one checked resident task**

For A or B, a sensible initial boundary is: choose an item → read a brief → distinguish stages/conditions → inspect exact evidence → understand the verified next step or absence of one.

The crucial work is preserving meaning across records. A sentence can have a genuine citation and still misrepresent it by omitting “if,” “subject to,” an exception, or the fact that it was only recommended. AI can draft extraction and explanations; someone must compare the meaning with the official records. A quote-presence check alone cannot do that.

Keep a small manually checked reference for each item: body, stage, date, proposal, final outcome where known, location, material conditions, uncertainty, source passages, and verified participation information. This gives you concrete acceptance checks rather than a vague claim that summaries seem accurate.

Try five revealing cases:

1. A committee recommendation must not be labelled final council approval.
2. An amended decision must preserve a material change or condition.
3. A past meeting must not produce a current comment invitation.
4. A missing fact must remain visibly unknown, with a source or verification route.
5. An unfamiliar person must find the answer and its supporting passage without coaching.

Ask a few people to try the same task with your prototype and the closest available alternative. Record completion, factual mistakes, uncertainty and time. A small convenience sample informs design; it does not establish population-wide accessibility or participation gains. Prefer an invited municipal mentor or someone familiar with the process for the consequential interpretation check.

A pilot would also need an owner for updates, stale-information handling, review effort and corrections. A reviewed print-friendly brief is one practical way to reach someone who will not use a web app. Use only the location/profile detail the chosen task actually needs, and avoid copying personal details from public correspondence.

**7. Allocate the remaining hackathon time**

| Period | Deliverable |
| --- | --- |
| First 90 minutes | Select one problem, obtain real inputs, choose the defining interaction. |
| Next 3–4 focused hours | Complete one real input-to-output path. Deploy early if using a hosted demo. |
| After a proper sleep block | Add the second and third cases; repair status, condition and citation errors. |
| Friday daytime | Get feedback, compare with the existing workflow, and check whether a first-time user succeeds. |
| Friday evening | Freeze feature scope; finish evidence checks, diagrams, report and demo rehearsal. |
| Saturday morning | Record/upload, check access while signed out, and submit with a buffer. |

The [published schedule and submission requirements](https://civic-hack-2026.devpost.com/) set the cutoff at **Saturday, November 14, 10:00 a.m. Eastern**. Plan against that time rather than the nominal “36 hours.” Mentor hours are Friday 10 a.m.–4 p.m. Required submissions are a repository, a 2–3 minute video, a one-page Municipal Readiness Report and a short description. All edits stop at the cutoff. Aim to submit by 9 a.m. and reserve at least the last three hours for submission work.

For two people, split source/reference work and implementation while integrating frequently; both should rehearse the full task. With three or four, assign explicit ownership of data verification, core processing, resident experience, and delivery. If solo, reduce interface breadth and automation before sacrificing the source checks or the completed task.

**8. Make the evidence easy to judge**

For the software discussion, define testable requirements, make UML diagrams match the built system, explain architectural trade-offs, and record dependencies and AI assistance. UML means standard diagrams of software structure or interactions; a sequence diagram can show the request from selecting an item through evidence retrieval to displaying an explanation. You need to understand what you built well enough to explain and debug it.

For the video, this suggested 150-second structure leaves room to show the contribution:

| Time | Show |
| --- | --- |
| 0–20 seconds | One person, a real item, and the question they cannot answer easily today. |
| 20–90 seconds | The complete task, including the defining interaction and a source passage. |
| 90–120 seconds | A revealing amendment, condition or closed-window case; explain what the tool correctly preserves. |
| 120–150 seconds | What you checked, what remains uncertain, and how a municipality could pilot it. |

For the one-page report, answer the municipal questions with concrete decisions: intended users and distribution, data and update owner, actual prototype evidence, review needs, rough running costs with assumptions, privacy, limitations, and adaptation to another municipality. Explain what stays reusable and what must change locally, such as source formats and participation procedures.

Your immediate task is to complete the first 90-minute session. Bring back the strongest problem statement, two official source links, and a sketch of the defining interaction. Those are enough to make the next design discussion concrete without committing to an entire product prematurely.
